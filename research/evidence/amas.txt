1. INTRODUCTION
Asteroid impacts have profoundly influenced the history of Earth, acting both as destructive agents and
as fundamental drivers of planetary and biological evolution. Throughout the age of the Solar System,
collisions with asteroids and other small bodies have repeatedly modified Earth’s geology, atmosphere,
climate, and biosphere. While impacts are often associated with catastrophic destruction, they have
also played a crucial role in shaping the conditions that enabled the emergence and evolution of life
[1].

1.1 Celestial Collisions: Driving Earth’s Evolution and Life
One of the most significant epochs in Earth’s early history was the Late Heavy Bombardment (LHB),
approximately 4.1 billion years ago. This period is believed to have been triggered by orbital reso-
nances between the giant planets, particularly Jupiter and Saturn, which destabilized large populations
of asteroids and sent them toward the inner Solar System [2]. During this era, countless impacts re-
shaped the surfaces of the terrestrial planets. Asteroids may also have delivered a substantial fraction
of Earth’s water and volatile compounds, contributing to the conditions necessary for the emergence
of life [3].
    Large impacts may have influenced biological evolution in ways that extend beyond simple de-
struction. Geological evidence associated with the S2 impact event, produced by an asteroid estimated
to be between 30 and 60 km in diameter approximately 3.2 billion years ago, suggests that asteroid
collisions could simultaneously generate severe environmental disturbances and chemically reactive
environments favourable to prebiotic chemistry [4]. While such events may have locally sterilized
portions of the Earth’s surface through intense heating and ocean vaporization, they also released nu-
trients and energy sources capable of stimulating biological activity.
    At the opposite extreme, asteroid impacts have repeatedly caused major biological crises. The most
famous example is the Chicxulub impact approximately 66 million years ago. The impactor, estimated
to have been 10–12 km in diameter, excavated a crater approximately 180 km wide on the present-day
Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico [5]. The resulting global environmental catastrophe contributed to the
extinction of nearly 75% of Earth’s species, including all non-avian dinosaurs.

1.2 Near-Earth Asteroid Impacts on Earth: Shaping Human Life and Culture
Asteroids have also influenced human civilization, both directly and indirectly. Beyond their geolog-
ical and climatic effects, celestial impacts have inspired myths, legends, religious interpretations, and
cultural narratives throughout human history.
    Although civilization has never experienced a Chicxulub-scale event, several recent impacts illus-
trate the continuing hazard posed by near-Earth asteroids (NEAs). The Tunguska event of 1908, likely
produced by a 50–80 m asteroid or cometary fragment, generated an atmospheric explosion equivalent
to approximately 15 megatons of TNT [6]. The blast flattened more than 2,000 km2 of Siberian forest.
    More recently, the Carancas meteorite impact in Peru in 2007 demonstrated that even relatively
small rocky bodies can survive atmospheric entry and reach the ground [7]. Although the object was
only a few meters in diameter, it produced a crater approximately 13 m wide.
    The Chelyabinsk meteor of 2013 provided another reminder of the threat posed by small near-Earth
objects (NEOs). The object, approximately 20 m in diameter and with an estimated mass of about
10,000 tons, exploded at an altitude of about 30 km with an energy release of nearly 500 kilotons of
TNT [8, 9]. The resulting shock wave injured more than 1,500 people and significantly damaged local
infrastructure. This event also highlighted the limitations of current survey programs in monitoring
objects approaching from Sunward directions.
    Today, planetary defence has become a major international scientific and technological priority.
Survey systems such as Pan-STARRS, Catalina Sky Survey, ATLAS, and the newly commissioned
Vera C. Rubin Observatory continuously monitor the sky, detecting and characterizing NEAs and
particularly potentially hazardous asteroids (PHAs)[10, 11]. Nevertheless, a significant fraction of
intermediate-to-small sized objects remains undiscovered or lost.
    The challenge facing planetary defence is therefore not merely the detection of hazardous objects,
but their detection sufficiently early to enable and implement effective mitigation strategies [12].


2. THE NEA DETECTION GAP
Current statistics indicate that the NEAs population is increasingly well characterized at large aster-
oids, but remains highly incomplete at smaller scales. According to the Minor Planet Center (MPC)
of the International Astronomical Union (IAU), more than 1.5 million small Solar System objects
(SSOs) have been identified, including over 42,000 NEAs (Table 1). Among these, there are only a
few hundreds objects with diameters more than 1 km, while several thousand are classified as PHAs
[13, 12].
    PHAs are defined as objects larger than approximately 140 m in diameter with a minimum orbital
intersection distance (MOID) with Earth of less than 0.05 AU, as such objects in case of Earth impact
will cause significant regional destruction.
    Despite significant progress in survey completeness, detection efficiency decreases rapidly for
smaller objects, particularly below 300m [12, 10]. Figure 1 illustrates the orbital distribution of some
known near-Earth objects and highlights several spacecraft missions dedicated to the exploration of
asteroids and comets.

    This distribution defines the so-called detection gap, i.e., the incomplete observational census of
sub-kilometre objects that still pose a significant risk to human society. While current surveys have
identified approximately 95% of kilometre-scale NEAs, the discovery completeness decreases rapidly
for smaller, but still potentially hazardous objects, reaching only about 40% for NEAs between 140 m
and 1 km and about 10% for objects between 45 m and 140 m, leaving approximately 90% of the latter
population still undetected.
    Several observational limitations contribute to this gap, including atmospheric extinction, weather
conditions, daylight constraints, limited sky coverage, and survey cadence limitations. These effects
introduce strong observational biases that limit completeness, particularly for fast-moving objects and
those with short visibility windows [10].
    Among these limitations, the most critical is the Sun-ward blind zone. Ground-based observatories
cannot observe regions close to the Sun due to solar avoidance constraints and increased background
brightness. As a consequence, asteroids approaching from solar directions often remain undetected
until shortly before close approach or impact [11].
    Historically, several objects illustrate this limitation, for example 2008 TC3 and 2019 OK, as well
as multiple other NEAs detected only days before their closest approach to Earth.
    Overcoming these constraints and blind regions remains one of the primary motivations for future
space-based and lunar survey architectures, which aim to enable sustained high-cadence monitoring
of near-Sun regions inaccessible to ground-based observatories.

3.1 Extended Warning Time from Lunar Geometry
NEAs observation is critical not only for planetary defence, but also for scientific characterization
and future resource utilization. Specialized survey systems are therefore required for both regular
follow-up monitoring and time-crucial alert observations.
    Given the average relative velocity of NEAs approaching Earth (≈ 20 km, s−1 ), the warning time
could be extended over a few hours for cases involving the detection of imminent impactors.
    Although this time interval may appear modest, it is scientifically and operationally significant
for planetary defence scenarios, particularly for small impactors where early warning is essential to
mitigate regional threats. Depending on the encounter geometry, lunar-based survey systems may pro-
vide up to 5–6 hours of additional warning time compared to Earth-based observatories. Importantly,
this gain occurs during the critical final-approach phase, when impact probabilities are refined, impact
corridors are constrained, and timely alerts and mitigation actions can be initiated.

Objects approaching from directions close to the Sun remain largely undetectable due to atmospheric

telescopes in these regions.

from Earth.

3.3 Why Specialized NEA Telescopes Are Critical
Planetary defence requires dedicated observational facilities optimized for the early detection and
follow-up of small or fast-moving objects. General-purpose observatories can complement these fa-
cilities by providing additional observations, increasing data coverage, and extending the orbital arcs
required for accurate orbit determination.
     The rationale for specialized NEA observatories can be summarized as follows:

    • Planetary defence: Early detection, impact probability assessment, and object characterization
      enable mitigation strategies for potentially hazardous impactors, particularly small NEAs (10–
      100 m), which are often detected only after close approach.

    • Dedicated survey capability: Specialized NEA observatories provide optimized cadence, sen-
      sitivity, and scheduling flexibility to detect and track small and fast-moving objects that are
      challenging for general-purpose observatories.

    • Scientific return: NEA observations provide key constraints on Solar System evolution, com-
      position, and dynamical history.

    • Resource potential: NEAs represent potential future sources for in-situ resource utilization and
      space economy development.

4. EARTH’S HARSHEST TESTBEDS
Before deployment on the lunar surface, next-generation NEA survey systems must be validated in
extreme terrestrial environments that provide operational analogues for autonomy, thermal stability,
optical and mechanical performances, and long-duration reliability.
    Desert sites such as the Atacama, Sahara, and Gobi offer exceptional atmospheric transparency and
low humidity, but impose strong thermal gradients and dust exposure, requiring sealed and thermally
stable instrument designs.
    High-altitude observatories in Mauna Kea, the Tibetan Plateau, and the Andes provide reduced
atmospheric absorption and excellent seeing conditions, while introducing constraints in accessibility
and environmental harshness that drive remote operation capabilities.
    Polar facilities, particularly in Antarctica (Dome A, Dome C, South Pole), represent the closest
analogue to lunar conditions, combining long-duration observing conditions and extended darkness
with extreme thermal conditions logistical limitations that necessitate full system autonomy.
    Together, these environments form a progressive validation pathway toward lunar deployment,
each addressing key constraints expected on the Moon, including thermal cycling, autonomy, environ-
mental sealing, and operational robustness. In summary, each class of site maps to a specific set of
technological challenges:

    • Desert sites: high optical quality balanced against dust exposure and pollution, and strong ther-
      mal gradients,

    • High-altitude sites: improved atmospheric performance at the expense of accessibility and
      operational constraints,

    • Polar sites: long-duration observing conditions and darkness under extreme isolation, tempera-
      ture and logistical limitations.

   The Moon offers a unique platform for NEA detection by extending observational geometry be-
yond Earth limitations, increasing early warning time by several hours, and enabling access to Sun-
ward directions that are inaccessible from the ground.
   This progression naturally leads from Earth-based analogues to lunar horizons, outlining the path-
way toward the next generation of planetary defence infrastructure.

5. ENGINEERING REQUIREMENTS FOR FUTURE LUNAR NEA TELE-
   SCOPES
Future lunar-based telescopes dedicated to NEAs detection impose a set of stringent engineering con-
straints driven by the need for long-term autonomy, environmental resilience, and high-cadence wide-
field observations. The following subsections summarize the main subsystems and their associated
requirements.

5.1 Optical System
A baseline concept consists of a compact wide-field telescope optimized for NEA survey operations,
combining wide sky coverage, deep sensitivity to detect small and faint objects, and high angular
resolution for precise astrometric measurements and impact probability assessment. Typical designs
consider apertures ranging between 0.5 and 1 meter, combined with a field of view ideally around 5
degrees in order to balance sky coverage, image resolution and detectability of faint objects.
    Such systems are primarily optimized for rapid sky surveys, enabling efficient detection of fast-
moving objects rather than detailed physical characterization. Optical stability, lightweight structures,
and minimal alignment drift are therefore key design drivers under lunar thermal cycling conditions.
Sun protection baffles are also required to ensure safe operation near the solar limb and to minimize
stray light contamination during low solar elongation observations.

5.2 Detectors
Detector systems must ensure high sensitivity and robustness under low-flux conditions. Key require-
ments include high quantum efficiency, low readout noise, and fast cadence imaging capabilities to
enable continuous monitoring of transient and fast-moving NEAs.
    Both visible and near-infrared architectures are considered. Infrared observations in the 3–5 µm
range are particularly attractive for detecting low-albedo asteroids, which are poorly constrained in vis-
ible wavelengths due to their reduced reflectivity. Radiation hardness and thermal stability of detector
arrays are also critical for long-duration lunar deployment.

5.3 Mechanical Design
Mechanical subsystems must be designed to operate reliably under abrasive dust exposure, vacuum
conditions, and repeated thermal expansion cycles. Long-term autonomous operation without human
intervention further increases the importance of structural robustness and redundancy.
    The system must include dust-resistant motorized protective hoods to prevent particulate contami-
nation during both idle and active operation phases. All optical and mechanical interfaces must rely on
watertight seals and abrasion-resistant coatings to ensure long-term durability under harsh lunar-like
conditions. Dry or cryogenic lubricants are preferred for all moving components in order to miti-


                                                   Page 10
M. Hadjara et al.                                                   Extreme-Environment NEA Telescopes


gate vacuum-induced degradation. In addition, optical fouling sensors are required to monitor dust
accumulation on critical surfaces in real time and trigger corrective actions.

5.4 Thermal Control
Thermal stability is a critical requirement for both optical alignment and detector performance. Poten-
tial solutions include multi-layer insulation, passive radiators optimized for deep-space heat rejection,
and controlled heating elements to stabilize sensitive components during extreme environmental cy-
cles.
     Phase-change materials may also be considered to buffer temperature fluctuations over long timescales.
The combination of passive and semi-active thermal control strategies is expected to provide the best
compromise between robustness, power efficiency, and long-term stability.

5.5 Software and Autonomy
Software autonomy constitutes a central pillar of system design and operational resilience. The tele-
scope control system must support autonomous non-sidereal tracking over a wide range of stable
angular rates, up to approximately 1.5 arcsec/sec, enabling observations of NEAs approaching from
different directions with varying apparent motions. In parallel, the astrometric reduction software must
perform precise centroiding measurements of trailed stars in the field of view (FOV) to ensure accurate
orbit determination.
    Robust self-recovery mechanisms, duplication of highly important nodes and subsystems are re-
quired, including cold reboot procedures, safe-mode transitions, and comprehensive anomaly logging
to ensure continuous operation in case of system failures. Communication constraints are simulated
through Moon–Earth latency models on the order of 2–3 seconds, requiring fully autonomous onboard
decision-making.
    Beyond these core functions, the system is expected to operate a fully autonomous processing
pipeline, from raw observations to MPC report submission, including real-time generation of alerts
for transient or potential impactors. This includes onboard detection, validation, and prioritization of
candidates without ground intervention.
    In addition, software autonomy extends to operational management, including observation schedul-
ing, real-time data reduction, and adaptive decision-making module based on system health and envi-
ronmental constraints.
    Energy-aware and battery-conscious operation is also an integral part of the software layer. Task
prioritization and computational load management must dynamically adapt to available power re-
sources, particularly during low-insolation periods or extended eclipse-like conditions, ensuring con-
tinuous scientific operation under constrained energy budgets.
    Consequently, artificial intelligence is absolutely essential for managing all the software- and
autonomy-related aspects mentioned above.

6. FROM FUTURE GENERATION OF SPACE TELESCOPES TO FUTURE
   LUNAR OBSERVATORIES
An alternative strategy for accelerating the development of lunar astronomy consists in leveraging the
technological heritage of previous space observatories. However, directly recycling existing telescopes
such as the Hubble Space Telescope for installation on the lunar surface is neither technically realistic
nor economically attractive. Such instruments were designed for operation in Earth orbit and would
require extensive modifications to withstand lunar dust, surface gravity, thermal cycling, and long-term
autonomous operation.
    A more practical approach consists of reusing proven technologies, subsystems, and engineer-
ing solutions developed for previous space missions while designing a new generation of instruments
specifically optimized for the lunar environment. This strategy allows the benefits of decades of tech-
nological development to be retained while avoiding the limitations associated with adapting legacy
systems beyond their original design specifications.
    Potential advantages include:

    • Leveraging heritage technologies and operational experience from previous space telescopes,
      thereby reducing development costs, technical risks, and deployment schedules;

    • Developing dedicated lunar-compatible technologies to address environmental and operational
      constraints, enhancing system robustness, autonomy, and long-term reliability.

    Within this framework, a particularly promising concept is the development of a standardized lunar
observatory platform, conceived as a sealed and autonomous ecosystem that protects scientific instru-
ments from lunar dust, vacuum exposure, and extreme thermal variations while providing long-term
operational support under lunar conditions. Such a platform would provide the common infrastructure
required by future telescopes, including thermal protection, power generation and storage, communica-
tion systems, structural support, stabilization capabilities, and autonomous control functions. Figure 3
presents the concept of a lunar-based observatory platform and illustrates a conceptual architecture for
a new generation of modular autonomous lunar telescopes based on a common infrastructure platform
supporting interchangeable scientific payloads.
    Rather than designing each observatory as a unique system, future instruments could be integrated
onto a common lunar platform capable of accommodating different payloads according to mission
objectives. This modular philosophy would significantly reduce development costs while enabling
rapid deployment of successive generations of telescopes.
    Such an approach would effectively establish a standardized "lunar observatory bus (or platform)"
analogous to the spacecraft bus concept widely used in satellite engineering. Future optical, infrared,
radio, or multi-wavelength instruments could then be developed independently and integrated onto this
common infrastructure. A generic modular lunar observatory architecture based on a sealed integrated
platform could therefore consist of:

    • Telescope or scientific payload module;

    • Power generation and energy storage module;

    • Communication and data relay module;
    • Autonomous control and artificial intelligence module;
    • Thermal regulation module;
    • Dust mitigation and environmental protection module;
    • Mobility or deployment module for future expandable systems.
    The combination of a stable lunar platform, sustained autonomous operations, and dedicated sur-
veys of low-elongation Sunward regions could therefore open a new observational domain largely
inaccessible to current ground-based facilities.

7. A GLOBAL EARTH–MOON PLANETARY DEFENCE NETWORK
The ultimate objective extends far beyond the deployment of individual telescopes. The long-term
vision is the establishment of an integrated Earth–Moon planetary defence network capable of contin-

                                               Page 13
M. Hadjara et al.                                                 Extreme-Environment NEA Telescopes


uously monitoring the near-Earth environment and providing early warning of potentially hazardous
objects. Such a system should integrate Earth-based observatories, lunar facilities, autonomous pro-
cessing centres, and AI-driven processing algorithms into a unified planetary defence ecosystem.
   This approach is based on the principle that no single observatory, whether Earth-based or lunar,
can provide complete coverage of the NEA population. Instead, a distributed network exploiting the
complementary advantages of multiple observing locations offers the highest probability of detecting
hazardous objects as early as possible and thus limiting their potential impact.

7.1 Earth Segment
The terrestrial segment would remain the foundation of planetary defence activities. Existing and
future survey facilities provide large sky coverage, mature operational infrastructures, and extensive
experience in automated asteroid detection.
    Representative facilities include:

    • Rubin Observatory;

    • Pan-STARRS;

    • ATLAS;

    • AMAS test-bed observatories.

    Ground-based observatories are particularly effective for large-scale sky surveys, rapid follow-
up observations, orbit refinement, and long-term monitoring of known objects. The AMAS network
described previously would additionally serve as a technology validation platform for future lunar
systems while simultaneously contributing to planetary defence observations.
    The geographical distribution of these facilities enables continuous monitoring over multiple lon-
gitudes and environmental conditions, reducing observational gaps caused by weather, daylight, and
seasonal effects.

7.2 Lunar Segment
The lunar segment represents the next development step in planetary defence infrastructure. By oper-
ating above Earth’s atmosphere and from a different observational geometry, lunar observatories can
access regions of the sky that are difficult or impossible to observe from terrestrial facilities.
    Potential assets include:

    • Near-side survey telescopes;

    • Polar observatories;

    • Infrared monitoring stations.

M. Hadjara et al.                                                     Extreme-Environment NEA Telescopes


    Near-side survey telescopes could conduct continuous searches for NEOs at low solar elongations.
Lunar polar observatories would benefit from unique illumination conditions and potentially stable
thermal environments, particularly near permanently shadowed regions. Dedicated infrared stations
would provide complementary measurements of asteroid thermal emission, improving estimates of
size, albedo, and impact energy.
    Particularly important is the ability of lunar observatories to detect objects belonging to populations
such as Atiras and other objects that spend much of their orbit close to the Sun from the terrestrial point
of view. Earlier detection of such bodies could increase warning times by months or even years in some
scenarios.
    In the longer term, multiple lunar observatories could be deployed as a distributed network across
different lunar latitudes and longitudes, providing redundancy, improved sky coverage, and enhanced
resilience against individual system failures.

7.3 Data Segment
The effectiveness of the Earth and lunar segments ultimately depends on the performance of the as-
sociated data infrastructure. Future planetary defence systems will increasingly rely on automated
processing pipelines capable of handling massive data volumes with minimal human intervention.
   Key components include:
    • Autonomous detection pipelines;
    • Orbit determination systems;
    • AI-assisted risk assessment.
    Autonomous detection pipelines would process observations in near real time, identifying moving
objects and filtering false positives. Orbit determination systems would continuously update orbital
solutions as new observations become available, allowing rapid refinement of impact probabilities.
    Artificial intelligence techniques could assist in candidate classification, anomaly detection, obser-
vation scheduling, and impact-risk evaluation. Machine learning algorithms may also help prioritize
follow-up observations by identifying objects with unusual dynamical properties or elevated hazard
potential.
    The integration of terrestrial and lunar observations within a common data architecture would
provide a substantial increase in orbital accuracy and detection completeness compared with isolated
observing systems.
    Ultimately, the network could evolve toward a fully autonomous planetary defence ecosystem ca-
pable of detecting, characterizing, tracking, and assessing potentially hazardous objects with minimal
delay between observation and issuing the alerts.

combining terrestrial observatories, lunar facilities, communication relays, autonomous processing
centers, and decision-support systems.
    Such a multi-layer architecture has the potential to significantly improve detection completeness,
orbital accuracy, and increase the warning times for hazardous objects. By combining the strengths of
terrestrial and lunar astronomy, it may constitute a major step toward a truly global planetary defence
facility for the twenty-first century.

8. CONCLUSIONS
NEAs remain both scientifically valuable targets and potential hazards for humanity. Over the past
decades, major advances in survey capabilities, detector technologies, and automated data processing
have dramatically increased the discovery rate of NEAs, and particularly potentially hazardous objects.
Nevertheless, important observational gaps persist for small and medium-sized asteroids approaching
from low solar elongations or from regions of the sky that remain difficult to observe from Earth.
    The growing awareness of planetary defence challenges highlights the need for complementary
observational strategies capable of extending current detection capabilities. In this context, lunar
observatories offer a particularly promising opportunity. Their unique observing geometry, absence
of atmosphere, and potential for continuous autonomous operation could significantly improve the
detection of objects approaching from the Sunward direction, including Atira-like asteroids that are
currently underrepresented in existing surveys. Earlier detection of such objects could translate into
substantially longer warning times and more effective mitigation strategies for the potential impact
scenarios.
    This paper has outlined a progressive roadmap toward such a capability. Rather than directly pur-

suing complex and costly lunar deployments, the proposed AMAS concept provides an intermediate
and pragmatic step based on terrestrial analogues. By exploiting the complementary environments
of the Atacama Desert, Inner Mongolia province of China, Antarctica, and the Sahara desert, criti-
cal technologies can be tested and validated under conditions that reproduce many of the operational
challenges expected on the Moon surface. These include thermal extremes, dust contamination, long-
duration autonomy, energy management, and remote control operations.
    The AMAS framework also offers benefits beyond technology validation. As a distributed interna-
tional network, it provides opportunities for scientific observations, operational training, international
collaboration, and the development of autonomous observing systems that can later be transferred to
lunar applications. The project therefore serves both as a planetary-defence demonstrator and as a
precursor to future lunar astronomical infrastructures.
    A key conclusion emerging from this study is that future lunar observatories should not necessar-
ily be conceived as isolated flagship missions. Instead, a modular architecture based on standardized
lunar platforms may provide a more sustainable and scalable approach. Such platforms could sup-
port successive generations of optical, infrared, and multi-wavelength instruments while benefiting
from common infrastructure for power, communications, thermal control, and autonomous operations.
This philosophy could substantially reduce development costs while accelerating the deployment of a
permanent astronomical presence on the lunar surface.
    Ultimately, the long-term vision extends toward the creation of a fully integrated Earth–Moon
planetary defence network. By combining terrestrial survey facilities, AMAS test-bed observatories,
future lunar telescopes, autonomous processing pipelines, and artificial intelligence-assisted decision
systems, such a network could provide unprecedented detection completeness, orbit determination
accuracy, and early-warning capabilities. Beyond planetary defence, this infrastructure would also
open new scientific opportunities in time-domain astronomy, infrared observations, and the study of
previously inaccessible populations of Solar System objects.
    As humanity enters a new era of lunar exploration and permanent cislunar (between Moon and
Earth) activity, the Moon is increasingly emerging not only as a destination, but also as a strategic
astronomical platform. The development of autonomous lunar NEA observatories, supported by ter-
restrial precursor networks such as AMAS, may therefore represent one of the most practical and
scientifically rewarding pathways toward the next generation of planetary-defence and astronomical
infrastructures.
