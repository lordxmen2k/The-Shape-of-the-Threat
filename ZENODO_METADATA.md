Title: The Shape of the Threat: Characterising Near-Earth Objects for Impact Accountability, and the Case for a Lunar Surface Array

Creator: Gerald Enrique Nelson Mc Kenzie
Publisher: Tico Internet LLC
Edition: 2026 First Edition
License: Apache License 2.0
DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24517244

**A near-Earth object catalogue entry carries no size, no mass, no shape and therefore no
energy and no hazard class, and the gap is not a detection gap.** As of 22 November 2026
the NASA/JPL catalogue held 43,212 known near-Earth objects, 11,725 of them larger than
about 140 metres and 883 larger than about a kilometre. The dangerous ones are already
mostly found: on the published characterisation figures, roughly 1,759 of those 11,725
objects have any physical account at all and about 9,966 have none, and NASA CNEOS
Sentry states that the impact energy it tabulates is generally good only to within a
factor of three because the mass is unmeasured. **For the hazard-relevant class the
gap is accounting, not discovery**, and the book says so rather than claiming a
detection capability that the platform does not have.

**The book works the chain that closes that gap — shape to size to mass to energy to
hazard class — from first principles, and carries its own arithmetic rather than only
citing other people's.** It re-derives the 2003 CAPS concept study's own baseline
table independently, reproducing the maximum-baseline column to within 0.71 per cent
and the precision column to within 2.21 per cent, and shows that the table's own
100-metre design point resolves a 50-metre object at 0.2 astronomical units across
0.70 fringe spacings: the instrument it describes is an astrometric centroider, not a
shape imager. It then states what shape imaging actually requires — four samples
across the diameter, which is a 321-metre baseline for a 140-metre object at 0.1 AU
and 3,740 metres for a 60-metre object at 0.5 AU — and draws the consequence that the
Moon is the only site where a kilometre-scale stable baseline can be built and held
still, because at that length on the ground the same baseline is simultaneously an
atmospheric, a metrological and a thermal-expansion problem.

**The newest result is the counterfactual, measured against the historical record
rather than against a model of it.** The record is in the NASA/JPL Small-Body Database
and Close Approach services: 2024 YR4 was catalogued on the night it passed at
828,775 km, and 2019 OK was announced three days before it passed at 71,355 km, both
because they arrived from the Sun-ward side. The honest conclusion is that the lunar
station does not improve discovery — it converts a detection into an account. Three
gains are measurable: the Sun-ward discovery window, reconstructed by inverting the
published lunar lead times into an implied ground limit of 0.102, 0.204 and 0.985
astronomical units for the 30-metre, 140-metre and kilometre classes; the optical
range to a non-cooperative target on the Earth–Moon baseline, good to 8.7 metres at
YR4's discovery-night distance and to about 13 centimetres at a 1.5-kilometre
baseline, a capability that exists nowhere else on Earth; and the size, at the same
encounters.

**The lunar claim is narrowed to what the arithmetic supports, and the book states
where it loses.** The mid-infrared channel loses to Sun–Earth L1 on cost and
complexity, because sunlit lunar regolith at 390 K radiates directly into the band in
which asteroid thermal emission is measured, and the book therefore carries no
mid-infrared channel. The station cannot survey: at 37.4 campaigns a year it covers
10.9 per cent of the annual arrival rate of the 140-metre class and would take 313
years to clear the known population. It is a targeted service selected by a rule, and
the rule returns the platform for a named target rather than an architecture. Every
headline number is produced by a single generator and re-derived at build time, and
the generated number set is printed in full in Appendix B so that a reader can check
it, change an assumption and re-run the argument.
